What you just watched

    

      
The lower row is the upper row again. Nothing else in that sequence can be predicted, because every character was drawn at random. So there is exactly one thing to learn, and for the first few hundred steps the model has not learned it.

      
Then it does, quickly. The light in the lower row is the model becoming sure of characters it has seen once, in a sequence it was never trained on. It saw only sequences like this one, random characters repeating at random intervals. It has no idea what English is. Type a repeating pattern into the third row and it continues yours too.

    

  


  

    

      
What an induction head is

      
    

    

      
It finds where this character came before, reads what followed and says that. This takes two heads, one stacked on the other. A head in layer 0 reads the previous character, so every position also carries what came just before it. A head in layer 1 then asks, at each position, where was the previous character the same as mine? It attends there and copies what it finds.

      
You can see both in the maps. Each row of a map is a position reading and each column a position being read, so the empty upper right is the future, which no head may read. The previous-character head draws a line just under the main diagonal. The induction head draws a parallel line 63 positions below it, in the lower half, because the earlier copy of a character is 64 back and the thing to read is one after that.

      
In the run saved with this page, seed 1 at step 3000, one head in layer 0 has a previous-character score of 0.98 and the four heads in layer 1 have prefix-matching scores from 0.87 to 0.94. This is the circuit Olsson et al. described in 2022. With one layer there is nothing beneath the head to say what came before each position.

      
The circuit is also why it stumbles, because it matches on the previous character only. Look at the last column of the lower row, which stays dark. The 4 before it appears three times in the first pass, followed by Z, U and b, and on seed 1 at step 3000 the model says Z at nearly 100%. The first column stays dark for a simpler reason: the b before it has never been seen.

      
Prompts go the same way. Where every character has one possible successor it is clean, so abcabcabcabcab gives cabcabc. After 1800 steps, the quick brown fox. the quick br gave ox. brox. quick. Read that one character at a time and every step is a real pair from the prompt: o was followed by x, and a space was followed by b. By step 3000 on seed 1 it gets that prompt right, own fox. the quick brown, and it still misses the 4 above.

    

  


  

    
Why this is a phase change

    

      
On the first pass through a random sequence the best possible loss is chance, ln 96 = 4.56 nats, however long you train. So the difference between the first pass and the second measures copying alone. That difference is the copy advantage. In the saved run it creeps up to 0.33 by step 350, reaches 1.73 by step 425 and stays above 2 from step 475. The remaining 2500 steps take it to 4.59.

      
The instruments come from Gunner Howe, arXiv 2609.19000. The event is copy advantage at or above 2 nats on two consecutive evals, reported at the second. The precursor is the first time a first-layer head reaches a previous-character score of 0.10. The prefix-matching score is the induction head's own signature.

    

  


  

    
What correct means here

    

      
The trainer is raw WGSL. The forward pass, the backward pass and AdamW are written by hand, with no ML library underneath. npm run parity checks the gradient of every parameter tensor against PyTorch autograd, and they agree to about 1e-6 relative L2. So what ran on your GPU computes the gradients PyTorch would.

      
The stronger test is a whole run. Started from the same weights on the same batches, this trainer and native PyTorch reached the event at the same evaluation on all 64 seeds of the study's two-layer model and on all 8 seeds run of the tab's own model. Their copy advantage never differed by more than 4.4e-6, one on a GPU and the other on a CPU.

      
It is not faster than native. On the M4 Pro this trainer takes 10.3 ms per step on the tab's model, PyTorch takes 5.5 on the same GPU through MPS, and 13.9 on the CPU with 8 threads. The point is that nothing needs installing.

      
Run at Gunner Howe's own configuration, far larger than the tab's, with Howe's language, anchor and event, this trainer reached the event at step 6225, inside the range Howe reports for 30 seeds, 5650 to 7400. The anchor fired at 0.847 of that time, against Howe's median of 0.844. It takes 24 minutes a seed, so this is a check on the window and the fraction, not a rerun of the fleet.

      
The configuration is 96 printable ASCII characters, 128 characters of context, width 128, 4 heads, 2 layers, batch 16. It trains only on uniform random characters made periodic.

    

  


  

    
What did not work

    

      
Gunner Howe's curriculum is too slow for a tab at this size. On a model of width 64 and context 64 it took a median of 7500 steps, against 575 for random characters repeating at random periods, which puts all the pressure on copying. The numbers are under Across seeds below.

      
Two negative controls showed no event in 6000 steps, on 8 seeds each. One layer has no head beneath it to read the previous character, and its copy advantage never passed 1.20. A model that never sees a repeat has nothing to copy, and it never passed 0.07.

    

  


  

    
Prior work

    

      
Other browser demos train induction heads live, including TheoremPath's lab and gama's Induction, both on the CPU or without a stated GPU backend. This one runs on the viewer's GPU in hand-written WGSL, keeps MLPs and LayerNorm, and streams previous-token and induction scores with head ablation on one page. We did not find that combination within our search coverage.

      
This stands on other people's work. Sequence Toy and Piston by Vin Howe train sequence models in the browser on WebGPU. TabGPT and webgpu-llm write their training kernels by hand in WGSL. Little Language Machine trains a small transformer client-side. The circuit is from Olsson et al. 2022, In-context Learning and Induction Heads. The instruments and the transition are from Gunner Howe 2026, arXiv 2609.19000.

    

  


  

    
Across seeds

    

      
This is a smaller model than the tab's, width 64 and context 64, so a rented CPU machine could run many seeds. Every configuration here ran 64 seeds unless it says otherwise, and the full tables are in the repo.

      
Most of the speed is the curriculum. On random characters made periodic this model reaches the event at a median of step 575. Gunner Howe's language made periodic takes 1025. That language with its original clone rule takes 7500, and 4 of 16 seeds had not got there by step 8000. Random characters under the clone rule never got there in any of 16 seeds.

      
      
Shared weights delay the event, and the delay grows with depth. A block here is one set of weights, so 8 layers with 1 block is the same block applied 8 times. At equal depth, fewer blocks means a later event, up to depth 8, where one block had not fired in 32 seeds by step 4000. Extra depth with its own weights costs little in steps. A filled circle is a model whose every layer has its own weights.

      
      

      
The precursor is a weaker clock on these small models than in Gunner Howe's fleet, where it fires at a median 0.844 of the time to the event and every seed falls between 0.735 and 0.901. Across the arms above the medians run from 0.73 to 0.85, and three sat just under that range, which fired a falsifier we had written down in advance. We guessed the cause was how close the 0.10 threshold sits to chance on a short probe. That was wrong. The cause on the two-layer arm is the evaluation clock: on an event near step 575, evaluating every 25 steps adds a real delay. New seeds evaluated every 5 steps read 0.756, and the same runs read at 25-step spacing give 0.727, the first study's median. Spacing does not explain Howe's curriculum on this model at 0.63, or 8 layers with 2 blocks at 0.68, so the fraction does not carry everywhere. A rescaled threshold we preregistered also missed on one arm, 0.903 against 0.901.

      
We did not find this delay reported within our search. The closest work finds looped models earlier than a dense model, on other tasks and at greater depth (2610.31421, 2610.24135), and we do not claim a contradiction.
